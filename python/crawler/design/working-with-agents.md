## Purpose: the yardstick for everything below

@weber, 2026-11-16: the bottleneck is the person, "exactly what large human organizations also face", and "THE core issue to resolve: Make sure the 'organization' is aligned with my goals, I stay in control, set the direction and supervise, but as much as possible is delegated to agents -- possibly even a decision tree of agents, with 'team leader' agents managing their team, and not me. … Get as much done as possible, which means being as economic as possible with my (and my colleagues) time, cognition and 'context window', while preserving alignment and quality of what we do and what we deliver."

So every option is judged by five questions: does it reduce what the person must read, decide and remember; does the person keep direction and control; do alignment and quality hold or improve; does the organisation keep itself running (its memory, know-how, upkeep), since that is "not an afterthought, but a key aspect" (@weber, 2026-11-16, section 7); and does it work beyond one person, for colleagues on the same projects and for other maintainers (section 8). The method (the kaizen plugin) is meant to "support me, support my colleagues, and support anyone who is interested".

## For people: the short version

- **Yes, the main session was overloaded.** Five days, 483 messages from @weber, 8 context summaries, ten areas in one head. The mistakes after summaries were mostly about *how to work* (where a command goes, check before claiming), and that is what research says summaries drop first: rules about how to work and what not to do vanish about six times more often than what to build (Salle et al. 2026).
- **What carried the work was written down**: standing rules, the decision log, BACKLOG, the ledger, the page. Every source agrees: state in files, not in a session.
- **Split by area, but keep one writer per area.** Parallel agents pay off for reading, research and review; parallel writers that depend on each other lose (Cognition, CooperBench, Google's scaling study). An "area agent" should be a file (notes, constraints, label), not a session that never ends.
- **The person is the limit, not the agents.** Coordination cost does not vanish with cheap agents; it moves to whoever integrates and decides (Brooks, span of control, Osmani's "orchestration tax"). Every structure must be judged by what it asks of @weber's attention.
- **Independence has to be built in.** Two people are independent by default; two agents briefed by the same session are not, and copies of one model share errors. A reviewer gets the artifact and the requirement, not the author's reasoning, and tests have the last word.
- **The closest human model is the Linux kernel**: area maintainers filter, the top decider sees about 1 % of patches, decisions are pushed down; plus mission command (intent, not method), checklists at fixed pause points, the andon cord, and structured handovers (SBAR).
- **A shallow tree of agents, with the person at the top only** (O10): team-leader agents decide within the person's intent and report exceptions; audits sit beside the tree and report to the person directly; the person supervises by sampling.
- **Each medium for its job** (section 7): git is the memory and the rulebook, issues the work and the conversation, messages the telephone, events the doorbell. Lessons are written cheaply but rarely reused (NASA's lessons system), so a lesson must become a rule, check or skill that loads by itself. Across projects (LiberTEM, EOSC, kaizen, devvm, modama), a portfolio map with every crossing pinned, tested and upgraded on purpose.
- **Beyond one person** (section 8): layers for the method, the team, each manager's portfolio and each person; most of it is proper project documentation, so people and agents onboard the same way; every privacy barrier is crossed by an editing step, and assistants to the manager take most of that editing, graded by risk.
- **Who is speaking is always clear** (O30): a bot never speaks as a person; it says it is a bot, who controls it, its affiliation and role; it holds only the credentials its role needs, and some acts stay reserved to people. Mistakes will happen; the controls bound their damage. Before certain credentials are used, a co-signer, as with four eyes at the institute (O31): an independent agent for bounded acts, the person only above that line.
- **Concrete next steps** are in section 6, the first three cheap enough for this week: rules that must hold go where summaries cannot drop them; a fresh session per area with a notes file; issues as briefs once the private GitLab exists.

## 2. What others do with coding agents (2025-2026)

Sources were read by a research agent on 2026-11-16; the ones marked (✓) were checked again by the main session the same day.

- **Anthropic.** Multi-agent research beat a single agent by 90.2 % on its internal evaluation, at about 15× the tokens of a chat; token use explained 80 % of the variance; coding has "fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research" ([multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Long-running work: "compaction isn't sufficient"; what worked was a progress file, git history and a feature list each fresh session reads first ([long-running harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Claude Code: `/clear` between unrelated tasks; a fresh reviewer "isn't swayed by the reasoning that produced the code", but "a reviewer that's asked to find problems will always find *something*", so say what to skip ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Agent teams: experimental, about 7× the tokens in plan mode, "start with 3-5 teammates", not for same-file edits ([agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams), [costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)).
- **What survives compaction** (✓ [context window](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/context-window)): the project-root `CLAUDE.md`, unscoped rules, auto memory and the plan are re-injected from disk; **nested `CLAUDE.md` files and path-scoped rules are loaded into the conversation when a file there is touched, "so compaction summarizes them away with everything else"**; a SessionStart hook on `compact` can add context back. The crawler's standing rules live in the nested `python/crawler/AGENTS.md`.
- **Cognition**, the sceptic: in 2025, "Don't Build Multi-Agents" (parallel sub-agents make conflicting implicit decisions). In 2026 (✓ [What's Actually Working](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working), Walden Yan, 2026-04-22): "multi-agent systems work best today when writes stay single-threaded"; a clean-context reviewer "go[es] deeper into areas the original coding agent may not"; the aim is "a coordinated system that scales human taste".
- **Practitioners.** Willison: "the natural bottleneck on all of this is how fast I can review the results"; code from your own spec is cheaper to review. Hashimoto: one agent at a time, parallel ones only for research and triage; after each mistake, a line in AGENTS.md or a tool "such that the agent never makes that mistake again". Beck: a plan file, TDD, structural and behavioural commits apart. Osmani (✓ [The Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/), 2026-05-24): "running multiple agents does not mean there is more of you"; the number of parallel agents "for most of us … is a low single digit"; "Only spend the lock on judgement."
- **Specs.** Spec Kit and OpenSpec make each step reviewable; Böckeler finds heavy specs costly ("I'd rather review code than all these markdown files") and sometimes ignored by the agent.
- **Other vendors** converge: one thread per coherent unit of work, a few hours at most, written acceptance criteria, the issue as the brief (OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot's agent, Devin).
- **Open-source policies**: a human understands and answers for every line; disclose tools and prompts; one scope per change; no unsupervised agents (EOSC Data Commons: soft limit 2,000 lines or 10 files; LLVM: "worth more to the project than the time it takes to review it"; Linux: agents never add Signed-off-by). QEMU, Gentoo and NetBSD refuse generated code.
- **Evidence.** METR's 2025 study: 19 % slower with AI, though developers believed they were 20 % faster; its 2026 update suggests a speedup it can no longer measure reliably, partly because people now run several agents. Faros telemetry: more PRs merged, review time up 91 %, no effect at company level. CooperBench: two agents building compatible features succeed about 30 % less often than one agent doing both. LLM reviewers flag correct code as wrong; running the proposed fix against tests is the check.

## 3. What human organisations know

From a research agent's reading (mostly encyclopaedic overviews plus the IIA, WHO, Google SRE, kernel.org and PEP 13 primary texts; quotes to be checked before print).

- **Structure trades specialisation against coordination.** A matrix (two lines of authority) spreads information but brings "accountability without control" and slow decisions. For agents: an area agent and a task agent both directing the same code reproduce dual reporting, silently.
- **Conway's law and Team Topologies.** Systems mirror communication structures; cognitive load caps what a team can own ("overloaded teams make poor decisions and move slowly"). An agent's context window *is* that load, measurable. If everything goes through one main session, the system will tend to have one hub; areas that talk through issues and stable interfaces push towards modules. Platform team = tooling and docs that shrink what each agent loads; enabling team = a skill.
- **Brooks' law, span of control.** Coordination grows faster than headcount. Cheap agents remove ramp-up cost, not integration cost; the binding span is how many agent results one person can judge per day.
- **Checks and balances.** Separation of duties, maker-checker, the IIA three lines (delivery; monitoring and challenge; independent audit, whose "independence from the responsibilities of management is critical"). Transfers best, since the checker is cheap; the risk is a checker briefed by the maker. Management override exists here too (the main session or the person can skip review), so a skip should be logged. RACI: an agent can be Responsible, never Accountable.
- **Mission command**: intent, constraints and what done means; method left to the doer. A good brief is that. "Trust earned over time" does not transfer: a fresh agent has no record, so trust rests on checks.
- **Crew resource management**: juniors must challenge the captain; for agents, the deference is to whoever wrote the brief, so ask explicitly for the case against.
- **Checklists** (WHO surgical checklist, 19 items at three pauses; failed where staff knew "neither why nor how"): pause points fit agents (before starting, before merging, before handing back), but each item should be a command that passes, not an attestation.
- **Toyota**: jidoka and the andon cord (any worker stops the line; the first pull calls the team leader, the line stops only if unresolved), genchi genbutsu, kaizen from logged stops. **Blameless postmortems** (Google SRE): fix the brief, tool or check, not "the agent"; "an unreviewed postmortem might as well never have existed".
- **Memory.** Transactive memory ("who knows what"), institutional memory and the bus factor. For agents every session end is total turnover: what lives only in a session has a bus factor of zero. SBAR (situation, background, assessment, recommendation) is a handback template; its known failure, "omission of detailed patient information", is our handoff failure.
- **Open source.** The kernel: subsystem maintainers, chains of two or three trees, Linus picks about 1.3 % of patches himself; one logical change per patch, each building; Reviewed-by and Tested-by tags. CODEOWNERS makes "the owner reviews" mechanical. PEP 13: the steering council uses its powers "as little as possible".

## 4. Humans and agents compared

From a research agent's reading (papers mostly at abstract level); the three studies marked (✓) were checked by the main session.

**Similar:** limited effective attention (about four chunks for people, Cowan; for models, quality falls with length, position and distractors: Lost in the Middle, RULER, NoLiMa, Chroma's "context rot"); going along with what one is handed (confirmation bias; sycophancy, anchoring); lenient self-assessment, and self-correction without outside feedback is unreliable (Huang et al.); early commitment that is hard to undo (models "get lost and do not recover" after a wrong turn, Laban et al.); memory that reconstructs rather than records.

**Different:**

- **Summaries drop rules first.** (✓ Salle et al., [arXiv 2610.31778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31778)) "Compaction can, however, silently remove task constraints"; in real sessions, instructions on how the agent should work and what it should not do are dropped about 6 times more often than specifications of what to build. (✓ Wang et al., [arXiv 2608.11242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11242)) compactors kept 17 % of side constraints; an inbox agent deleted e-mails without approval in 30 of 100 sessions after compaction, none before; keeping constraints apart and adding them back raised retention above 90 %.
- **Memory files are instructions, not experience.** (✓ Gloaguen et al., [arXiv 2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988)) context files "do[] not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20%"; useful for non-standard conventions, not overviews.
- **Personas do little.** Two studies found no accuracy gain from "you are an expert in X" (Zheng et al.; Basil et al.); one found gains for zero-shot reasoning (Kong et al.). Specialists pay off through what they load, which tools and permissions they have, and what they answer for.
- **Multi-agent depends on the task's shape**: from +81 % on decomposable work to −70 % on sequential planning (Kim et al., Google); 14 failure modes in three groups, design, misalignment between agents, verification (Cemri et al.).
- **Copies share errors.** Models agree 60 % of the time when both are wrong, more within one vendor (Kim et al., ICML 2025). A team of copies is less diverse than a team of people; a different model as reviewer adds diversity.
- **New abilities.** An agent's state can be dumped, copied, forked, restarted from a brief, moved to another machine; fresh eyes cost almost nothing. But no experience accumulates between sessions except in files and in the model. Cost scales with tokens, not hours; the person's attention does not scale.

## 5. Diagnosis: why the long session degraded

1. **Overload by areas, not by length alone.** Ten areas share one context; every step re-reads all of them (460 k tokens per step on average), and the facts of one area are distractors for the next (context rot, Chroma).
2. **The rules that broke are the kind summaries drop.** Where a command goes, check before claiming, times from `date -u`: all *how to work*, all in the conversation or the nested `AGENTS.md`, both summarised away (Salle; Wang; Claude Code's compaction table). Auto memory survives compaction, but the newest lessons were in the ledger, not in memory.
3. **Sentences, not checks.** The rules that held were hooks (no inline edits) or tools (`trace`, `status`). The ones that broke were sentences.
4. **One hub for everything.** All coordination went through the main session and the person (Conway): every briefing, every merge, every relay to @weber. The hub's span is the bottleneck.
5. **Independence partly engineered.** Reviews got code and requirements (good); but the same session briefed maker and checker, relayed findings in its own words, and fresh testers inherited the parent's instructions.

## 6. Options

Each with what it would have changed in this session. Leans marked; nothing decided.

- **O1 Rules where summaries cannot drop them** (lean: now, small). Move the rules that must always hold into the root `CLAUDE.md` or a file that a SessionStart hook on `compact` re-adds; turn the ones a check can enforce into hooks (a page write without a `date -u` in the same batch; a command for the person outside the final message is harder). Keep the nested `AGENTS.md` for area conventions. *Here:* the invisible commands and the claims after summaries would have had their rules in view.
- **O2 A session per area, state in files** (lean: from the next working day). Areas, following what this session carried: staging operations; EM formats; common formats; search chain (harvester, warehouse, search, AI mode); requirements and design; upstream and the split. Each area has a notes file (state, open questions, next steps; the progress file of Anthropic's harness) and, later, an issue label; a session starts by reading it and ends by writing it (SBAR-style). A small coordinating session merges, keeps the page or board, and decides only across areas. *Here:* the 15 Nov staging check would have started with the operations notes, not 90 MB of everything; the 8 summaries would have been a few short sessions.
- **O3 One writer per area, many readers and reviewers** (lean: yes, it is what worked). Research, review, hallway tests and what-if forks run in parallel; code in an area is written by one agent at a time. *Here:* the commit that swept up another agent's deletion and the shared `commit-msg.txt` would not have happened.
- **O4 Issues as briefs, in the private GitLab** (lean: after the demo, as agreed for 379 to 381). An issue holds intent, constraints, done-when, and the area label; it outlives any session; labels such as `needs-weber` and `blocked` replace the page's status. *Here:* 532 page writes become comments on issues; the page's answer boxes become replies, which also notify.
- **O5 Independence by construction** (lean: yes). The reviewer gets the diff and the requirement only, is briefed by a session that did not write the code, reports into the record (a `Reviewed-by:`-style trailer naming the reviewing agent and model), is told what to skip, and tests decide its findings; for code that deletes data or touches security, a reviewer on a different model. A skipped review is logged. *Here:* the item-123 relay in shorthand would have been the reviewer's own plain report.
- **O6 Budget the person's attention** (lean: yes). A work-in-progress limit (Osmani: a low single digit of parallel streams that need judgement); decisions batched on the board at fixed times; every question arrives with ways to find the answer and options with examples (@weber, 11-15); the andon in two pulls: ask first, stop the line only if it stays open. *Here:* fewer "how should I do X?" messages; blockers visible without scrolling.
- **O7 Checklists at three pause points** (lean: yes, short). Before starting (brief read, area notes read, worktree made), before merging (suite as CI runs it, review, docs, BACKLOG), before handing back (notes written, worktree removed, numbers with their source). Each item a command where possible. *Here:* 76 worktrees and 30 open changes would have a place to be closed.
- **O8 Upstream as curated series owned by a person** (lean: as in `project-split.md`). One logical change per merge request, building, tested, with tools and prompts disclosed as the target project asks (EOSC Data Commons, LLVM, Linux); the person signs and can defend every line.
- **O9 Specialists by what they load, not by a persona** (lean: yes, when O2 exists). An area's agent definition names its notes, constraints, tools and permissions (for example a read-only staging agent once the MCP server exists, items 370 to 373), not "you are an expert in EM formats".

- **O10 A tree of agents, with the person at the top only** (lean: yes, shallow; @weber's direction of 2026-11-16). Team-leader agents own an area, brief and review their own workers, and decide within the intent the person set; the person sets intent, priorities and area boundaries, answers escalations and appeals, and supervises by sampling. Design points, each from section 3 or 4:
  - **Shallow.** At most two levels below the person (person → area leader → workers): the kernel's chains "rarely exceed two or three links", and every level is a handoff that loses detail and a summary that drops rules. Google's scaling study found errors amplified 4.4× under a central orchestrator against 17.2× with independent agents: a leader helps, but each layer still compounds.
  - **Intent down, exceptions up.** Leaders get commander's intent (goal, why, constraints, done-when) and report by exception: a decision outside the intent, a blocker after the first pull of the andon, a risk. Routine results go into the record, not to the person (PEP 13: use the top's powers "as little as possible").
  - **Audit beside the tree, not through it.** Independent review and hallway tests are briefed from the record, not by the leader whose work they check, and report to the person or the log directly (the IIA's third line answers to the governing body, not to management). This is how the person stays in control without reading everything.
  - **Supervision by sampling.** The person looks at a few results in depth (go and see), picked by the audit or at random, rather than skimming all of them; the leaders' decisions are in the decision log, where the person can overrule.
  - **One digest, one queue.** What reaches the person is a short digest per day and one queue of decisions, each with ways to find the answer and options with examples; the leader prepares, the person picks.
  - **Measured.** The person's minutes per merged change, decisions asked per day, and audit findings that the tree missed: if these do not improve, the tree is overhead.
  - *Here:* this session was a tree of depth one with the person as the only leader above the main session, and the main session as the only leader of 162 agents; the main session's context was the span of control that broke.

Against splitting, the strongest argument: every handoff loses detail, and today the main session is where @weber's preferences and the connections between areas come together. With areas, that integration needs a home of its own: the coordinating session's narrow job, the decision log, and @weber.

## 7. Persistence, work across areas, and the organisation running itself

@weber, 2026-11-16: "How do we manage persistence and cross-team collaboration in practice? E.g. here we saw significant overlap between LiberTEM and the crawler, despite them being rather different projects. That's how I understand matrix organizations. Are git repos a good way to preserve state and transfer information? And/or messaging? … the 'organization' also needs to effectively manage itself and be effective and efficient. So that is not an afterthought, but a key aspect … We need also horizontal transfer of know-how, preservation of what one agent found interesting and relevant so that other agents can benefit from that. Also, message and event passing."

### What we saw

- **Overlap across projects.** The crawler drew on LiberTEM again and again: its readers as the reference for formats (K2IS sector files, `.blo` bit depth, EMPAD, Direct Electron test data), its thread-setting code copied under its MIT notice for the CPU budget, its CPU count that ignores containers (a fix drafted for LiberTEM), LiberTEM-schema for units; and RosettaSciIO and the EOSC stack the same way. The one place this know-how met was @weber's head and the main session's context: the matrix's crossing point was a person and a session, not a record.
- **What persisted.** Git (code, specs, decision log, BACKLOG, ledger, field-test findings F1 to F17) survived every session and summary. The page persisted, but answers on it did not notify anyone. Messages between agents (181 in this session) and anything said only in chat did not survive the session or the summaries.
- **Events.** The only events that started work by themselves were the crawl timer and the harvest it triggers; everything else waited for @weber to type, or for an agent's completion notice inside a live session. "Work launched in a session dies with the session" (`/docs/working-with-claude.md`).
- **Know-how that stayed local.** Lessons found by one agent reached others only if the main session wrote them into a rule, a skill or a brief: the NFS buffered-read finding (F12), "own instruments first", the float-versus-integer round trip (392). Many stayed in a sub-agent's report.
- **The organisation's own upkeep** had no owner: 76 worktrees, 30 open changes, a memory index and a ledger that grow, a page with 298 items.

### Which medium for what

| What | Medium | Why | Not for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decisions, rules, conventions, distilled know-how (skills, checklists) | Git, reviewed | durable, versioned, diffable, reviewable, travels with a clone, works offline and for any tool | anything that changes hourly; waiting and notifying |
| Work items, questions, discussions, who waits on whom | Issues (GitLab), linked across projects | addressable, outlive sessions, notify, carry labels and assignees, an API agents can use, links between repositories | reviewed knowledge (it gets buried in threads) |
| Live coordination inside a task | Messages between agents | fast | anything that must outlive the task: write the outcome back to the issue or the record |
| Something happened (a crawl ended, a review finished, an issue got a label, a run failed) | Events: CI, webhooks, schedules, the monitor log | start work without a person typing | memory: an event is gone once handled; log it |
| Raw state for a handover (logs, a session's full trace) | Files next to the work, linked from the issue | an agent's state can be dumped completely and cheaply | the reader's attention: always with a short summary on top |

So: git is the memory and the rulebook; issues are the work and the conversation; messages are the telephone; events are the doorbell. Git alone is too slow and too reviewed for coordination; messaging alone forgets.

### Options

- **O11 One shared know-how record across projects, pulled not pushed** (lean: yes, small first). A findings register with stable IDs and tags (format, library, storage, performance, process), in the style of the field test's F1 to F17, kept in one place that crawler, LiberTEM and later projects all link to; each entry short: what, evidence, where it applies, link. Agents search it at the start of a task in its area; nobody broadcasts. Distilled know-how graduates into a skill or a library (the formats-em package in `project-split.md` is the architectural form: the shared code itself carries the shared knowledge). *Here:* the CPU count in containers and the K2IS layout would be one entry each, found by whoever touches them next, in either project.
- **O12 A librarian role for the record** (lean: yes, as a scheduled agent). Merges duplicates, prunes stale entries, keeps memory and instruction files short (more lines lower adherence: Claude Code keeps 200 lines of memory; Gloaguen et al. found context files raise cost), and promotes repeated lessons into rules, hooks or skills. ### Checked against sources (398)

A fourth research agent read these on 2026-11-16 (report kept with the others); (✓) marks what the main session checked again.

- **Lessons-learned systems fail at reuse, not at writing.** GAO (2002): NASA's lessons were "not routinely identified, collected, or shared"; NASA's Inspector General (2012): project managers did not routinely search the lessons system or add to it, finding it outdated, hard to use and not relevant, "of diminishing and questionable value". The US Army's after-action review guidance gives the follow-up (retraining, revised procedures) a chapter of its own. Agents write everything down cheaply; finding and using it is where both fail. So a lesson becomes a changed rule, check, test or skill that is loaded automatically (O11, O12), not an entry in a register that waits to be searched. This sharpens O11: the register is the inbox, the rule or check is the result.
- **Communities of practice and guilds** carry know-how across teams (Lave and Wenger; Spotify's chapters and guilds, "the glue that keeps the company together", in Kniberg and Ivarsson's 2012 paper). Spotify is a weak precedent: a former Spotify coach is quoted, "Even at the time we wrote it, we weren't doing it" (Jeremiah Lee, 2020). For agents the guild is a shared skills or know-how repository every agent loads; it needs a keeper (O12).
- **Lateral links cost, in rising steps** (Galbraith, as summarised by the Corporate Research Forum): informal networks, management processes, teams, integrator roles, a full matrix, in rising "Cost, Management Time, and Difficulty"; and information crossing a boundary tends to get biased and distorted (Tushman). Use the cheapest step that works (a shared document, a linked issue) before a liaison role (O15), and never let the person become the matrix.
- **Blackboards and event logs** (shared board with specialists that act when it matches; Fowler's event sourcing, "Capture all changes to an application state as a sequence of events") are being tried for LLM agents again in 2025-2026 preprints, with the authors' own results only. They fit forgetful, forkable agents: a new agent catches up by reading the log. The hard part stays the control (who acts, who settles conflicting writes), and a person reads the log only through a summary.
- **Issue trackers for agents exist.** (✓ [Beads](https://github.com/steveyegge/beads), "A memory upgrade for your coding agent"): tasks with blocking dependencies, `bd ready` lists "tasks with no open blockers", atomic claims, `bd remember` and `bd prime` store project facts and inject them into the agent's context; its `issues.jsonl` "is an export … not the source of truth". Claude Code's agent teams have a shared task list with file-locked claims (experimental). A2A connects agents to agents, MCP an agent to tools; A2A matters only for agents run as remote services. For us: GitLab issues with labels and links are the human-readable tracker; whether agents need a dependency-aware layer on top (ready, claim) is a question for O4's trial.
- **Programme management** (UK government standard GovS 002, 2025): record the dependencies between components, but first "Dependencies between work components should be minimised to make each component as self-contained as possible". For O16: minimise the crossings, then pin and list the rest.
- **One repository or many**: no neutral evidence. Google's monorepo depends on custom tooling; critics say a monorepo needs discipline that most teams lack, while many repositories hide maintenance costs. The case that agents work better in a monorepo comes from a vendor (Nx). Upstream projects (EOSC, LiberTEM) cannot be merged in anyway, so pins and linked changes across repositories (O17, O19) are the realistic middle.
- **Release trains** (Linux's two-week merge window, Chromium and Firefox every two weeks; SAFe's trains of 50 to 125 people): work that misses the cut waits for the next train. A fixed cadence for bumping pins across projects lets agents integrate continuously while the person reviews in batches at known times (O6, O17); SAFe's ceremony does not fit one principal.

Not checked by anyone: the original books (Lave and Wenger, Nonaka, Galbraith), Nii on blackboards, PMI and MSP texts, Mozilla's 2019 change of cadence.

### Project documentation is the persistence

Most of what an agent needs to work on a project is what a new colleague needs: what it is, how it works, how to build and test it, the conventions, the decisions and why, what is next. Written as proper project documentation (README, developer docs, `CONTRIBUTING`, the decision log, the tracker), it serves both, survives every session, and is reviewed like code. The agent-specific layer can then stay thin: an `AGENTS.md` with only what agents need beyond the docs (`python/crawler/AGENTS.md` already says "Read `README.md` first … This file only adds what is specific to agents"). The evidence agrees: context files that repeat an overview do not help agents and cost tokens; what helps is the non-standard conventions (Gloaguen et al.). Onboarding is then measurable the same way for both: a fresh agent, and later a colleague, follows the docs from a clean clone to a first change (the hallway test of 2026-11-13 did this for the crawler's developer docs).

### Privacy barriers are editing steps

Each layer boundary is crossed by editing, not by copying: what goes from the personal layer to the team, or from the team to upstream, is "shortened, edited and rearranged". Our crossings so far, and who edits at each:

| From → to | What crosses | Who edits | Example here |
|---|---|---|---|
| inner monologue (chat with agents) → private working record | decisions, findings, open items | the agent drafts, the person keeps or drops | the decision log, BACKLOG, the ledger, out of 483 messages |
| private working record → team | conventions, rules, shared know-how, issues others need | the person, with review by the colleagues | "Team rules, moved here from one person's agent memory" (2026-11-13) |
| team or private repository → upstream | curated series, issues, reports | the person signs; the agent drafts, split for people and agents | O8, 381: series rebuilt from the final tree, not the 622 commits |
| anything → public docs | what a newcomer needs | review | the developer docs, after their hallway test |

Rules that follow: nothing crosses a barrier without the editing step and the person whose layer it leaves; the editing step is where private context (names, inner reasoning, half-formed ideas) is filtered out; the record keeps a link back only where the receiving side may follow it.

  - **Identity.** A bot never speaks as a person. A fix in git's own terms: the agent is the commit's author under a bot identity, the person who merges is the committer, and only the person signs (the Linux rule: agents never add Signed-off-by; LLVM's `Assisted-by:`).
  - **Four eyes before a credential is used: O31.**
  - **Limited credentials, by role.** Like signing authority in a company (a procurist may sign much but not everything; some acts are reserved to the board): each bot gets only the credentials its role needs (a project token at Developer level, protected branches, merge only through review, no access to the person's own accounts), and a short list of acts is reserved to people whatever any bot is told: anything that spends money, deletes data, publishes or posts outside the team, grants access, or speaks for the institute. - **O31 A co-signer before certain credentials are used** (lean: yes, tiered; @weber, 2026-11-16, names replaced: "most actions in FZJ are 'four eye principle'. So [a colleague] and I can order a poster ourselves without asking [our group leader] (keep his load low), but we can't do it on our own. That means there's accountability and a cross check. The impact of ordering a 'stupid' poster is relatively low, so it is OK to accept the risk that [a colleague] and I may both be on the wrong track since the cost of [the group leader]'s time is much higher than the cost of the odd 'stupid' poster. Also, [the group leader] can't properly judge if the poster is stupid or not without loading all our context (expensive). The main benefit is to mitigate gross abuse, and to make us think about justification in the first place -- if I want a poster, I need to talk with [a colleague] about that. So does it make sense to have a 'four eye check' with an independent agent before certain credentials are used?").
  - **Why it fits.** The four eyes here are peers, not the boss: the check is cheap, catches gross errors and abuse, and forces a justification to be written at all; the boss's attention is saved for what is above the line. For agents there is a third benefit: an agent that read untrusted content (files in the data tree, an upstream issue, a web page) can be steered by it; a co-signer that sees only the request, its justification and the policy, not that content, is a firewall against such instructions.
  - **Tiers**, by the cost of a wrong act against the cost of the check: (1) routine and easily undone (a commit on a working branch, a draft): no check, logged; (2) bounded impact (merge into a team branch, a token used to open an issue in our own tracker, a staging restart): an agent co-signer, logged, the person samples; (3) larger impact (anything touching production, other people's data, money, or what users see): the person co-signs, prepared as one decision with the justification; (4) reserved (O30's list): the person acts, or two people.
  - **How, so it holds.** The credential is held by the system, not by the requesting agent: the tracker's approval rules, protected branches, a token broker that releases an act only with a second approval from a different bot account (like a two-person rule). A co-signer that the requester could skip is a sentence, not a check (section 5, point 3). The co-signer is briefed independently (O5), sees the request, the justification, the policy and the diff, not the requester's reasoning, and ideally runs on a different model, since copies of one model share errors (section 4).
  - **Strongest argument against.** An agent co-signer is a weaker peer than a colleague: it shares failure modes with the requester when both are the same model, tends to agree with a well-argued request, and cannot be held accountable. So tier 2 is about gross errors, abuse and justification, as at the institute, not about judging whether the poster is good; whether it was is seen later, by sampling and in the debrief. And every tier-2 approval is logged with both identities (O30), so a pair of bots on the wrong track leaves a trail.
  - **A gatekeeper, not a peer** (@weber, 2026-11-16: "Spawning agents and onboarding them with a specific context is very cheap and they don't cost money when they are waiting. So instead of peer review like in a human org where real people play several roles … in an agent org we can have an independent reviewer/gatekeeper role even at the working level. Also, there's to my knowledge no such thing as an access token with two halves in practice. So instead of peer review we can add a check step where only an independent 'gatekeeper agent' has access to credentials. They receive a request with a brief (e.g. please open PR; title and description; local and upstream branches; transcript of independent agent review), perform consistency check and check against own and upstream's rules, and reject with reason and required improvements, or grant request and perform the action."). People switch between doing and authorising because a dedicated checker costs a salary; an agent that only checks costs nothing while it waits. So the working agents hold no credentials at all; one gatekeeper per kind of act holds them and acts only on a request that passes. This replaces the token with two halves that API tokens do not offer (multi-party approval exists for some cloud actions, as the research report on human organisations mentions, but not for a GitLab token).
    - **What it checks, against the artifacts, not the brief's claims:** the branches exist and the diff is the one described; the review transcript is for this commit and its findings are answered; the title and description match the diff and follow the two-audience split; the receiving project's rules (its AI policy, size limits, disclosure) and ours (O30's identity, O28's filters: no colleagues' names, no internal host names); the act is in its allow-list.
    - **What it answers, three ways:** granted, and it performs the act under its own bot identity (O30) and logs it; rejected, with the reason and what to improve, back to the requester (one round trip, not the person's attention); or, where policy reserves the act to a person (O30's list, the receiving project's rules), escalated (@weber, 2026-11-16: "there's a third path: Prepare a brief for me and request me to perform the action"). The escalation is a prepared decision in the person's queue (O6): what to do and why in a few lines, the exact act (the command, the pull request text, the button), what the gatekeeper checked and found, and what is left for the person's own judgement; the person reads it, acts under their own name, or sends it back. The check is done before it reaches the person, so the person's time goes to judgement, not to checking.
    - **What keeps it safe:** it has only the tools for its acts (open a pull request, merge, restart staging), not a shell; it reads the request's text as data, never as instructions (a pull request description written by an agent that read untrusted content could carry injected instructions); its rules live in git and only the person changes them (tier 4); the person can revoke its token at any time; where it may not act on its own, it simply holds no write token for that project (@weber, 2026-11-16), so escalation is the only path it has, enforced by the credential, not by its rules; it runs on a different model from the requesters where possible.
    - **Gatekeeper and assistant (O29): overlap, but two roles** (@weber, 2026-11-16: "it feels like this overlaps a lot with the 'assistant to the manager' role, right?"). Shared: both prepare briefs for the person, both apply the same filters (names, internal host names, the two-audience split), both grade by risk. Different: the assistant sits on the person's side of the privacy barrier, reads the private record and drafts (the maker); the gatekeeper holds the credentials, checks and acts (the checker), and should not read the private record, both to stay independent of the maker and to stay out of reach of instructions hidden in what the maker read. Merged, they would be maker and checker in one, the thing separation of duties exists to prevent; as with an executive assistant who prepares a payment and a second signatory who releases it. With agents cheap and free while waiting, keeping them apart costs little. What they share is written once and used by both (the filter list, the brief format, the tiers), and the gatekeeper's escalations land in the queue the assistant keeps for the person. In @weber's words (2026-11-16): the gatekeeper is "more like an 'authorized officer' -- directly under my authority, but separate from assistant/secretary". So it reports to the person directly, not through the assistant or a team leader (as audit reports to the board, section 3), takes its rules only from the person, and its authority is exactly the credentials and allow-list the person gave it.
    - **Where it stops:** it moves an act from "the person does it" to "the person samples it" only where O30's reserved list allows. Opening a pull request upstream is posting outside the team, reserved to people today, and the receiving projects ask for a person: EOSC Data Commons allows no "fully unsupervised agentic tools", LLVM wants a human in the loop who has read everything, Linux has the person sign. So upstream, the gatekeeper prepares and checks, and escalates with a brief for the person to open or sign (the third answer above); inside the private repository (merges, internal pull requests, staging), the gatekeeper acts.

## 8b. Method: recognising isomorphisms

### What the research says

From a research agent's reading on 2026-11-16 (report in `working-with-agents/research-analogy-and-reuse.md`); (✓) marks what the main session checked again on the arXiv pages, where only the abstract's direction was checked, not the numbers from the full texts.

- **Partly true: the weak step is noticing, not mapping.** Once an analogy is named, models map one domain onto the other fairly well; far analogies are clearly harder than near ones on every benchmark the agent read (ARN: "even the largest ones struggle with far analogies"; SCAR: "LLMs often overlook the structures"). Webb, Holyoak and Lu (Nature Human Behaviour 2023): GPT-3 found the analogous solution to the radiation problem after the fortress story, lost it when two distractor stories were added, and found it again only when told to consider the earlier stories.
- **Humans show the same.** In Gick and Holyoak's classic setup, about 30 % of people used a distant analogue spontaneously and about 80 % after a hint (as summarised by Pedone, Hummel and Holyoak 2001); people retrieve by surface similarity but judge by structure (Gentner and colleagues). For GPT-4 the same protocol gave almost no recognition without a hint and full recall with one, but also many wrongly applied analogies (✓ Puranam, Sen and Workiewicz, [arXiv 2505.00603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00603): "AI errors arise from surface level matching"; one preprint with small numbers).
- **"Excellent within an established ontology" is overstated.** Models are also brittle on variants within one domain (Lewis and Mitchell; Hodel and West), though Webb et al. dispute the cause. The dispute is open.
- **Our gitignore case was a reuse failure, not an analogy failure**: the analogy had been named. On reuse the evidence goes both ways: agent pull requests duplicate existing code more than human ones (✓ Huang et al., MSR 2026, [arXiv 2601.21276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21276): "LLM Agents frequently disregard code reuse opportunities"; one repository), while models over-use popular libraries elsewhere (Twist et al. 2026). The research agent's hypothesis, not a finding: agents reuse what they associate with the task's own words (`glob`, `fnmatch`) and miss what is named in another field's words (`pathspec`, "gitwildmatch") or already in the repository. Not-invented-here is measured as an attitude in people (Hannen et al. 2019); nothing shows such an attitude in models, so the label describes the outcome, not a shared cause. The inner-platform effect is folklore without studies.
- **What helps, in people and models:** a hint (the main lever for both); stating the principle first ("step back": ✓ Zheng et al., ICLR 2024, [arXiv 2310.06117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06117)); comparing two cases side by side rather than one at a time (Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson 2003); describing the parts of a problem without the words that imply their use (McCaffrey's generic-parts technique; one small study); a shared name for the abstraction, which is what catalogues such as design patterns give (their evidence is weak, but the name is the retrieval cue the studies say is missing). No study was found that tests agents on recognising an existing protocol or library from a description in another field's words.

- **O37 Fuzz both sides and look for an intersection** (lean: develop as a kaizen skill, after O36's first result; @weber, 2026-11-16: "one could 'fuzz' by using a thesaurus to replace words with same or similar meaning, invert it, use logic operations in the ontology (e.g. reverse direction, like in 'contains' vs. 'is part of'), and try to match fuzzed versions with the world around and known things", and "try to match parts of it only, … all sorts of variants we can generate. Also, perhaps keep a library of commonly used 'things' such as tools, libraries, …, with a pre-cooked ontology to also fuzz, in order to try to get an intersection"). Two halves:
  - **The problem, fuzzed:** restate it with synonyms and more general terms; invert relations and operations (skip ↔ include, contains ↔ is part of, find ↔ publish so others can find, reader ↔ writer); generalise, specialise, compose from two simpler problems, take the other party's view; and split it into parts and match each part alone (McCaffrey's generic parts: a partial isomorphism is still a reuse, as the crawler reuses gitignore's syntax but not git).
  - **The known things, described the same way:** a library of commonly used tools, libraries, standards, formats and protocols, each with a short structural description in neutral words (what it relates, which operations, which direction, which constraints: gitignore = ordered patterns over paths, later wins, negation, anchoring; ruff selection = prefix ids, a default set, exceptions both ways; OAI-PMH = a provider publishes records with datestamps, harvesters pull increments, deletions as tombstones), fuzzed the same way. A match is wherever a variant of the problem meets a variant of a known thing; candidates come with which variants met and where the fit breaks.
  - The equivalence catalogue (O32) is the project's own slice of that library. Arm B of the O36 experiment tries the first half by prompt alone.

## 9. What does not transfer

- Trust built over time (mission command, maintainership, deference to expertise): a fresh agent has no record; trust rests on checks and the written record.
- Long-term memory in people: every handover is total turnover.
- Social limits and remedies (Dunbar, morale, blame, rank deference): agents have other biases (agreeing with the brief, over-asserting), which need other remedies (independent briefs, asking for the case against).
- Cost structure: ramp-up and a second reviewer are cheap for agents; any added decision point for the person is dear.
- Independence through separate people: agents must be made independent; copies share errors.
- Accountability: only people can be accountable (RACI's A, a signature, Signed-off-by).
